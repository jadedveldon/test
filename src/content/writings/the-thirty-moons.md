The Hindu calendar counts days by the moon as well as by the sun. A **tithi** is a lunar day: the time it takes the moon to move twelve degrees ahead of the sun. There are thirty in each lunar month, so a tithi does not line up neatly with a calendar day. It can begin in the morning, at night or in the middle of the afternoon.

## Two fortnights

The month is split into two halves, each called a *paksha*.

- **Shukla paksha**, the bright fortnight, runs from the new moon to the full moon while the moon grows.
- **Krishna paksha**, the dark fortnight, runs from the full moon back to the new moon while it wanes.

The tithis in each half are counted from one to fourteen: Pratipada, Dwitiya, Tritiya and so on up to Chaturdashi. The fifteenth is the turning point.

## The nights that matter

- **Purnima**, the full moon, ends the bright fortnight. It is a night for gratitude, completion and fullness.
- **Amavasya**, the new moon, ends the dark fortnight. The sky is dark and the veil is thin. It is the night most sacred to Kali, and the great Kali Puja falls on the Amavasya of Kartik.
- **Ashtami** (the eighth) and **Chaturdashi** (the fourteenth) of the dark fortnight are also strong nights for the Devi.
- **Ekadashi** (the eleventh) in each fortnight is widely kept for fasting and prayer.

## The sky on this site

The moon you see behind these pages is tonight's moon, drawn from its real phase. The little moon at the top of each page tells you the tithi. On Amavasya the whole site grows darker and redder; on Purnima it turns silver.
